# Writing That Works

Principles of effective business writing distilled from *Writing That Works* by Kenneth Roman and Joel Raphaelson. These guidelines apply to all professional communication — emails, memos, letters, presentations, plans, reports, proposals, resumes, and speeches.

---

## Core Philosophy

The goal of business writing is not literary beauty — it is effective communication. Writing that gets the reader to understand, decide, and act. Every sentence should earn its place by moving the reader closer to the action you seek.

> "Too many of the communications I get are meaningless. They don't help me understand what action the writer wants me to take. They waste my time." — A leading CEO

**Before writing, answer three questions:**

1. What do I want the reader to do?
2. What are the three most important things the reader needs to understand to take that action?
3. If I were the reader, would I take action based on what is written?

## The 18 Principles of Effective Writing

### 1. Don't mumble
Say what you mean directly. Don't tiptoe around your point. Vague, hedged writing invites the reader to tune out. If you have something to say, come right out and say it.

### 2. Make your organisation clear
Start by telling the reader where you are going. Use an outline. Number and label section headings. End with a summary that introduces no new ideas — it recaps your most important points. For long documents, open with an executive summary.

### 3. Use short paragraphs, short sentences, and short words
Short units are easier to read and understand. Prefer common words over fancy synonyms:

- "Now" not "Currently"
- "Start" not "Initiate"
- "Use" not "Utilize"
- "Speed up" not "Expedite"
- "Show" not "Indicate"
- "Finish" not "Finalize"

If you can express your thinking in simple words, you have probably distilled it to its essence.

### 4. Make your writing active and personal
Choose active voice over passive voice whenever possible. Active verbs add energy. The passive voice hides who is speaking; the active voice reveals it.

- Passive: "It is recommended" → Active: "We recommend"
- Passive: "He should be told" → Active: "Get Alice to tell him"

Use "I," "we," and "you." A human being talking is more engaging than an institution issuing pronouncements.

### 5. Avoid vague adjectives and adverbs
Lazy modifiers like "very good," "great success," and "basically accurate" are cliches that say nothing. Be precise instead:

- "Very overspent" → "Overspent by $10,000"
- "Slightly behind schedule" → "One day late"

Vigorous modifiers sharpen your meaning: "crisp presentation," "baffling instructions," "tiny raise," "tiresome speech."

### 6. Use down-to-earth language
Avoid jargon, buzzwords, and technobabble. There is always a plain-English alternative:

- "Interface" → "Discuss, meet, work with"
- "Resource constrained" → "Not enough people (or money)"
- "Suboptimal" → "Less than ideal"
- "Net net" → "Conclusion"
- "Push the envelope" → "Test the limits"
- "Operationalized its goal" → "Achieved"

Write the way you talk — at your best. If you wouldn't say it aloud to your reader, don't write it. (Exception: if your talk has become impenetrable jargon, fix that first.)

### 7. Be specific
Generalities are the fatal weakness of business writing. The reader shouldn't have to take your claims on faith. Replace vague assertions with concrete evidence:

- Vague: "Our program was a great success. We attracted more students than ever before."
- Specific: "Our enrolment doubled to 560. Students came from 28 states, Germany, and Canada."

Specifics speak for themselves.

### 8. Choose the right word
Know the precise meaning of every word. Commonly confused pairs include:

- affect / effect
- its / it's
- imply / infer
- principal / principle
- definite / definitive
- disinterested / uninterested
- alternate / alternative
- fortuitous / fortunate

When you confuse words like these, your reader may conclude you don't know any better.

### 9. Make it perfect
No typos, no misspellings, no errors in numbers or dates. Slipshod details — however minor they seem to you — make the reader question how much care you put into your thinking. Spell-checkers help but are not infallible. Proofread with your own eyes.

### 10. Come to the point
Boil down what you want to say and express it confidently in simple, declarative sentences.

- Mumbled: "The situation in regard to France is very serious."
- Direct: "The news from France is bad." (Churchill)

The Gettysburg Address is 266 words. "Jesus wept" is two.

### 11. Write simply and naturally
Your writing should sound like you talking at your best. Don't pad a ten-word spoken message into a 24-word written version:

- Spoken: "If you need more manuals, just ask for them."
- Written (badly): "Should the supply of manuals sent you not be sufficient to meet your requirements, application should be made to this office for additional copies."

Match your tone to the reader — more formal for the President, less for your colleague — but it should still sound like you.

### 12. Strike out words you don't need
Cut relentlessly:

- "Advance plan" → "Plan"
- "Hold a meeting" → "Meet"
- "New innovations" → "Innovations"
- "At this point in time" → "Now"
- "In the majority of instances" → "Usually"
- "Despite the fact that" → "Although"
- "For the purpose of" → "To"
- "In the event of" → "If"
- "Until such time as" → "Until"

### 13. Use current standard English
Don't pioneer in usage. Established usage offends nobody; new usage offends many ears. Follow conventional grammar. Common errors to avoid: "like" where "as if" or "as though" fits; using "I" instead of "me" as an object ("He asked Helen and I" is wrong).

### 14. Don't write like a lawyer or a bureaucrat
Long sentences, clauses within clauses, abbreviations, and jargon are symptoms of "bureaucratese." If you catch yourself writing this way, put down what you want to say the way you'd say it face-to-face, then adjust the tone.

### 15. Keep in mind what your reader doesn't know
Never expect readers to read your mind. Consider how much background they need, which technical terms require explanation, and which abbreviations might be an indecipherable code. Define abbreviations the first time they appear.

### 16. Understate rather than overstate
A single exaggeration can arouse suspicion of your entire case. Round numbers conservatively: call 6.7 "over six and a half," not "nearly seven." As Mencken said: "Don't overdo it."

### 17. Write so you cannot be misunderstood
It's not enough to be understandable — guard against being misunderstood. Ambiguity often results from sentences carrying too much cargo. Break them up. Read with your reader's eyes.

### 18. Use plain English even on technical subjects
The more technical the material, the more you need plain language — unless writer and reader share the same specialty. Test early drafts on lay readers. Never hide bad news behind jargon or euphemism; readers detect deception and their displeasure is compounded by feeling you tried to slip one past them.

## Editing Yourself

Never send the first draft of anything important. Edit to: shorten, sharpen, simplify, check accuracy, improve order and logic, review tone, and examine everything from the reader's point of view.

### Editing Checklist

1. **Cut ruthlessly.** First pass: ask only "What can I get rid of?" Mark Twain: "Strike out every third word. You have no idea what vigor it adds to style."
2. **Are you mumbling?** Have you chosen verbs and adjectives that express your meaning precisely? Could you be less abstract?
3. **Is the order right?** Good writers shift things around a lot. Print drafts; compare side by side. Sometimes a reordering you thought was an improvement turns out not to be.
4. **Are there holes in the argument?** Put yourself in the reader's shoes. Does everything follow logically? Don't expect readers to leap like goats on a hillside.
5. **Are the facts right?** A single bad error undermines confidence. Always check quotations — even when you're certain, you're probably wrong.
6. **Is the tone right?** Too stiff? Too chummy? Lacking sympathy? Rude? Change anything a reader might find offensive.

### Two Simple Editing Tips

- **Let time elapse between drafts.** Come back in the morning with fresh eyes.
- **Ask someone else to read it.** Nearly everybody spots something you overlooked.

## Format-Specific Guidelines

### Email

- **Make the subject line clear and compelling.** It's the headline of your email — the thing that converts scanners into readers. Be specific: "Winning client approval" beats "Status of proposal." Never leave it blank.
- **Cut to the point — literally.** Try removing 50% of what you've written. Anything over one screen risks not being read. Chip away everything that isn't your point.
- **Avoid email tag.** Provide context. "I'm available" should be "I'm available to speak at your meeting on the fourteenth." Be clear about what you want the reader to do. Set deadlines.
- **Set the right tone.** Email is faceless and voiceless — brief comments can seem abrupt, terse questions can seem angry. A greeting ("Hello George" or "Hi") and a warm close ("Thanks," "Best wishes") remove doubt about your state of mind.
- **Compose yourself, then compose your message.** If angry, sleep on it before clicking Send.
- **Limit copies.** Don't broadcast. If you want action, list only one name in the TO: field. Count to ten before clicking Reply All.
- **Nothing is private.** Never write anything in email you wouldn't want on the front page of the newspaper.

### Memos

- **Title every memo.** Centre it in capitals. Make it identify the subject swiftly: "RAISE FOR TONY ANDRINO" not "LONG OVERDUE."
- **Address only the person who must act.** Copy those who merely need to know. List copy recipients alphabetically.
- **Make structure obvious.** Any memo longer than half a page needs visible structure — numbered points, headings, or an outline.
- **End with a call to action.** Say exactly what must be done, by whom, by when.
- **Be careful with humour and anger.** If angry, write it but don't send until the next day. Avoid irony — somebody will take it straight.

### Letters

- **Get names and addresses right.** Misspellings get you off on the wrong foot.
- **Think about the salutation.** Use first names only when you're on a first-name basis. "Dear Joan Larson" is an attractive middle ground.
- **Make the first sentence work hard.** No small talk. Your reader wants to know at once what the letter is about.
- **Stop when you're through.** Don't add routine formalities like "Please call if you have any questions." If your last sentence says what the reader would assume anyway, leave it out.
- **Be specific about next steps.** Vague: "We're hoping to hear from you soon." Specific: "Please let us know your decision by August 1."
- **Saying no:** Be as clear, tactful, and understanding as you would be in person. Show you appreciate the reader's predicament. Explain your reasons. Never belittle the request. Leave channels open.
- **Complaints:** Put the complaint and desired action in the first sentence. Include everything needed to take action (account numbers, dates, copies). Be firm and courteous.

### Presentations

- **Keep it simple, keep it on target.** Open with a headline theme. Tie every element to it.
- **Tell the audience where you're going.** Show an agenda, state the time you'll take, overestimate slightly.
- **Talk about them, not about us.** Start with the audience's problems, not your credentials.
- **Use headlines, not labels.** Chart headings should tell the audience what the data means: "Low price competition is gaining" not "Trends."
- **Read everything on the screen to the audience — then comment.** If they're reading one thing and hearing another, they'll be confused.
- **Involve the audience.** Games, predictions, tape recordings, unexpected elements. But relevance over entertainment.
- **Finish strong.** Summarise. Restate your recommendation. Keep your time promise. Leave time for Q&A.

### Speeches

- **Decide the single point** you want the audience to take away. Then start writing.
- **Frame the subject** with a personal point of view.
- **Start fast** — plunge into your message. Cut the first several paragraphs; your real opening is often halfway down page one.
- **Write to be spoken, not read.** Think conversation with a friend.
- **Leave them thinking** about a fresh perspective.
- **No speech was ever too short.** Most good talks are under twenty minutes.
- **Rehearse** until you know it almost by heart. The better you know it, the more spontaneous you'll sound.

### Plans

- **Start with a clear statement of purpose.** What action do you seek?
- **Build on rigorously selected facts.** Draw conclusions. Show how facts bear on proposed action. Never present facts like "unstrung gems" — they must link together and come to a point.
- **State recommendations clearly with reasons.** Consider alternatives and risks. Face problems squarely; don't cover them up.
- **Make it a call to action:** leave no doubt about where you stand.

### Reports

- **Make clear why you're writing.** Engage the reader's interest in the first sentence.
- **Give it structure:** Purpose → Summary → Findings → Conclusions → Recommendations → Next steps.
- **State facts fully and accurately** (who, what, when, where, why). Get out of the office and see for yourself. Never trust your memory — write things down.
- **Separate opinion from fact.** Both are important; make clear which is which.

### Recommendations

- **Think of it as selling, not presenting.** Marshal logic and passion behind your facts.
- **Tell people where you are going** in the first paragraph. Include background.
- **Lead people through with headings.**
- **Recommend early.** No surprise endings. Get the recommendation up front, then lay out specific supporting reasons. Each specific must be relevant and impressive — a chain is no stronger than its weakest link.
- **Emphasise benefits.** Never fail to answer: "What's in it for me?"
- **Anticipate questions.** If you can't build answers into the presentation, be ready to handle them briefly and respectfully.

### Proposals (for grants/funding)

- **Get to the point fast** — how much money, for what.
- **Show how your project meets their stated goals.**
- **Organise to persuade** — everything relevant, nothing extraneous.
- **Make it urgent** — convey that the project won't happen without funding.
- **Invest more time improving the product** than improving the proposal.
- **It need not be dull** — use colourful detail and arresting openings.

### Resumes

- **Lead with a summary** of what you have to offer — experience, skills, results. Put it in boldface at the top. Think about this more than anything else.
- **Keep it simple.** Standard format, one to two pages. No fancy fonts, coloured paper, or gimmicks.
- **Describe accomplishments as results, not activities.** How far you moved the rock matters more than how long you pushed it.
- **Be ruthlessly selective.** Leave out the small stuff and old stuff. Everything should point to why you'd be good at this job.
- **Make it perfect.** No typos. No misspellings. A small error sends the wrong signal.

### Cover Letters

- **Think about the reader's needs, not your virtues.** Start with what you can offer them.
- **State clearly what job you want** and what led you to apply.
- **Be specific and factual,** not abstract ("Ambition mixed with striving for excellence" — no).
- **Be personal and natural.** Sound like yourself across a desk.
- **Keep it to half a page,** a page at most.
- **Propose a specific next step:** "I'll call your office Wednesday afternoon."
- **Tailor each letter** to each employer. Follow up interviews in writing.

## Making It Easy to Read

Visual presentation matters. If your document looks formidable, your reader braces for an ordeal before reading a word.

- Start with a heading — top centre, capitals.
- Keep paragraphs short. Break long ones in two.
- Use typographic emphasis sparingly — italics for emphasis, boldface for headings only. When everything is emphasised, nothing is.
- Numbered or bulleted points help readers follow your thinking.
- Use uppercase and lowercase, not ALL CAPS (which reads as SHOUTING).
- Break up large blocks of text with subheads and white space.
- Use space between paragraphs rather than indents.
- Handle numbers consistently.
- Number your pages, even in early drafts.

## Quick Reference: The Writer's Checklist

Before you send anything important, verify:

- [ ] Is the purpose clear in the first paragraph?
- [ ] Is there a specific call to action?
- [ ] Is it organised logically with visible structure?
- [ ] Have I cut every unnecessary word?
- [ ] Am I using active voice and personal language?
- [ ] Are claims supported with specifics, not generalities?
- [ ] Is the tone right for this reader?
- [ ] Have I avoided jargon and buzzwords?
- [ ] Is every fact, name, number, and date correct?
- [ ] Does the subject line / title compel attention?
- [ ] Would I take action if I received this?
- [ ] Have I let it sit and come back with fresh eyes?
